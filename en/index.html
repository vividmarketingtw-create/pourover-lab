Getting Started
Start with your first cup and build your own pour-over rhythm
There is no single right answer in pour-over, but there is a path that saves you detours. Below is the learning path, the essential gear, and the jargon you may hear but never quite understood.

Three-Stage Learning Path

🌱 Level 1  ·  Beginner
Brew a good first cup, consistently
Recommended: Single Pour · James Hoffmann
Get your movements steady — no need to chase perfection. Learn the bloom and a steady pour rhythm first, and get a feel for what a decent cup tastes like.
Goal: practice 5–10 times and note the taste each time
🧪 Level 2  ·  Exploration
Start adjusting and find your flavor preferences
Recommended: Kasuya 4:6 · ultra-high sweetness
Learn to steer flavor with ratio, water temperature, and grind size. Try the same beans with different methods and feel the personality of each.
Goal: be able to say "I want more sweetness / more acidity / more body"
🎯 Level 3  ·  Mastery
Master advanced techniques, chase repeatable great cups
Recommended: Scott Rao · Immersion · Melodrip
Dive into the details of extraction yield, grind uniformity, and flow control. Brew with a specific experiment goal every time, and learn to reproduce a cup you love.
Goal: control every variable deliberately

Equipment Guide
Starter Equipment Recommendations

Dripper

Hario V60 (site default) — cone shape with spiral ribs; fast flow and highly controllable. Works with every method on this site.
Kalita Wave — flat-bottom, three-hole design; steady flow and a wide margin for error, great for beginners.
Origami — takes cone or wave filters, the most versatile option.

Grinder

Beginner pick: hand grinders (e.g. C2, 1Zpresso Q2) — even grind, excellent value.
Advanced pick: electric conical burr grinders — more consistent, for precision-minded brewers.
Key principle: grind uniformity matters more than price; burr sharpness affects flavor clarity.

Kettle

Gooseneck kettle (recommended) — precise flow control, ideal for pulse pouring.
Crane-spout kettle — wide flow range, suited to Single Pour or strong-stream methods.
Temperature-control kettle — set an exact water temperature and skip the thermometer.

Scale

Basics: 0.1g precision with a built-in timer.
Advanced features: auto-timing, flow-rate display, Bluetooth app connection.
Key reminder: the scale matters more than the thermometer — dose and water accuracy directly shape flavor.

Water Science
How Water Quality Affects Pour-Over
Coffee is 98% water — water quality directly determines extraction efficiency and final flavor, yet it is the most overlooked variable.

TDS (Total Dissolved Solids)

Ideal range: 75–150 ppm
TDS too low (soft water): weak extraction — thin, sour-leaning flavor.
TDS too high (hard water): hinders extraction, adds bitterness and metallic notes, and speeds up scale buildup.
Taiwan tap water runs about 100–200 ppm; mineral waters vary widely — choose products with TDS < 150.

Magnesium vs Calcium

Magnesium enhances sweetness and fruit acidity, helping extract bright aromatic compounds.
Calcium lowers extraction, giving a balanced but potentially duller cup.
High-magnesium, low-calcium water (some mineral waters) especially suits light roasts; medium-dark roasts are less sensitive to water.

pH

Recommended pH 6.5–7.5 (near neutral)
Acidic water (pH < 6): boosts acidity and can make light roasts too sharp.
Alkaline water (pH > 8): mutes acidity and smooths medium-dark roasts, but too high adds an alkaline taste.
Filtered water is usually near neutral — no special treatment needed.

Practical Advice

Simplest choice: tap water run through a Brita pitcher — TDS drops into range and pH stays near neutral, plenty for daily pour-over.
Advanced players: try low-TDS mineral water (Volvic, or diluted Évian) and compare the same beans across different waters — a fun experiment.

⚗️ SCA brewing water standards: TDS 75–175 ppm, total hardness 17–85 ppm, sodium < 30 ppm, pH 6.5–7.5, odor-free and chlorine-free. Remove chlorine by letting water sit 30 minutes or using a filter pitcher.

Flavor Language
Understanding Coffee Flavor

The SCA (Specialty Coffee Association) flavor wheel is the shared language for describing coffee. Learning to identify these flavors helps you fine-tune your brewing parameters with precision.

Floral / Fruity

Jasmine, rose, citrus, berries, tropical fruit. Typical of light roasts and the stars of early extraction. Higher water temperature and shorter brew times make them easier to taste.

Sweet / Caramel

Honey, caramel, brown sugar, maple, chocolate. The core flavors of mid-extraction, most prominent in medium roasts. The swirl technique and slightly lower water temperature help highlight sweetness.

Nutty / Cocoa

Almond, hazelnut, peanut, cocoa, toasted bread. Signature flavors of medium-dark roasts, usually with a full body. A 1:14-1:15 ratio reinforces these notes.

Spice / Herbal

Cinnamon, clove, mint, vanilla. Common in special processes (like anaerobic) or beans from specific origins. These flavors often become clearer as the cup cools.

Tip: taste in three stages — the first impression on entry (acidity, florals), the mid-palate body (sweetness, fruit), and the finish (lingering sweetness, chocolate notes). As the coffee cools, different flavors surface in turn.
